## Season first, region second

Most people planning a wine journey start with "which estate." Anyone who truly knows the world of wine starts differently: "which season." The same château gives you a completely different experience in April than it does in October. The vine keeps its own calendar, and every estate moves with it — what you'll actually see, whether you'll be properly received, and whether the wine itself is even the right thing to open that day, all comes down to where you land in that calendar.

## Four seasons, four completely different estates

![Close-up of a cluster of dark grapes hanging on the vine in Vougeot, Burgundy.](/images/journey-bordeaux-burgundy-vines-detail.jpg)

**Spring (April–May): budbreak, the quietest point in an estate's year**

The vines have just woken from dormancy, the first buds are only beginning to show, and there's none of the high-season bustle — winemakers have more time to sit down and talk with you. This is a good season for a quiet visit, for seeing how an estate is preparing for the new vintage, and it's also the easiest time to secure a tasting hosted personally by the estate's owner.

**Summer (June–August): the vines at their fullest, made for outdoor tasting**

The vineyard is at its greenest, days run long, and many estates move tastings into the evening, paired with dinner outdoors on the property. This is the season to blend a wine itinerary with a genuine holiday rhythm — no need to rush between stops; spend the day by a pool or in a garden, and head out once evening comes.

**Harvest (September–October): the most ceremonial point of the year — and the most in demand**

Known in French as the _vendange_, this is the single most important moment on the wine calendar. Some estates allow guests to take part in picking, or even watch the pressing. But it's also the estate's busiest stretch, and quite a few simply won't receive visitors for tastings during this window — booking often needs to happen months, sometimes half a year, in advance, with dates shifting to match how that year's harvest is actually progressing.

**Winter (November–March): settling into the cellar, for serious tasting**

The quietest season for visitors turns out to be the best one for depth — with no high-season rush, an estate has real time to sit with you, open an aged bottle, and tell you the story behind that particular vintage. A fireside dinner at an estate, too, tends to only carry that particular atmosphere in this season.

## November in Burgundy: the Beaune auction weekend

If one date in the Burgundy calendar justifies planning around, it is the third weekend of November, known as Les Trois Glorieuses: the chapter of the Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin at Clos de Vougeot, the Hospices de Beaune wine auction, and the Paulée de Meursault.

The auction is the centre of it. The 166th Hospices de Beaune sale takes place on Sunday 15 November 2026 at the Halles de Beaune, and this year's charity barrel is dedicated to research on rare diseases. Beaujolais Nouveau follows on Thursday 19 November, the third Thursday of the month.

A few practical points. The dates are fixed, so rooms in and around Beaune are the first thing to settle. The dinners and the chapter at Clos de Vougeot have their own invitations, which we do not arrange; what we can plan is the hotel, a driver for the weekend and a day or two with estates on either side of it, at the quieter pace the rest of the winter allows. Dates for the Vougeot chapter and the Paulée are set by their organisers, so we confirm them with you at booking.

## Different regions, different rhythms

![A vineyard near Bordeaux with a village church in the distance.](/images/destination-bordeaux.jpg)

**Bordeaux**: the Left Bank leans Cabernet Sauvignon, the Right Bank leans Merlot, and the contrast between the two banks makes for a natural comparison within a single trip. The châteaux themselves tend to be grander in scale, suited to a somewhat more formal style of tasting.

**Burgundy**: just two varieties, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, yet parsed into a finely layered classification — village level, Premier Cru, Grand Cru — where two neighbouring plots along the same Route des Grands Crus can produce wines with entirely different personalities. Estates here, the domaines, tend to be smaller and more family-run, with tastings often taking place in the winemaker's own home.

![Vineyard rows with a farmhouse in Lourmarin, Provence.](/images/destination-provence-vineyard.jpg)

**Provence**: if the itinerary could use a lighter pace, Provence's rosé tradition is worth including — rather than the "deep tasting" mode of Bordeaux or Burgundy, this is better suited to an estate lunch with a chilled glass of rosé, a breathing space within the broader wine journey.
