There are two things families dread most when travelling with children: an itinerary packed too tight for kids to keep up with, and a "family-friendly" label that turns out to be an adult experience scaled down, with kids bored the whole way through. When we select or design family experiences, there are four specific things we check.

## Match the activity to the age, not a generic "family activity" label

- **Under 5:** attention typically holds for 20–30 minutes of high-intensity explanation at most — better suited to activities with room to wander, stop and start, with something physical to see or touch, and commentary that's short and concrete.
- **5–8:** can follow a simplified narrative but still needs frequent interaction to stay engaged — pure listening loses attention fast.
- **9–12:** largely able to participate like a small adult, though the pace should still run a little slower than an adult-only group, and building in interactive moments still helps.
- **Teenagers:** tend to respond better to an itinerary with some room for their own choices, rather than being fully scheduled — a little "pick your own" space makes a visible difference in engagement.

Our private family museum morning in London is built around this — the guide confirms the children's ages first and sets the depth of commentary accordingly.

## Does the guide actually engage with the kids

A museum visit led by an adult-oriented guide and one skilled with children plays out very differently — the latter asks questions, leaves room for kids to touch and observe, rather than reciting dates and artists by rote. How well a family experience is designed usually shows more clearly in how the guide interacts than in the itinerary description.

## Pacing and timing need to be honest, including meals and rest

We won't take a three-hour adult itinerary and hand it to a family with one line added — "suitable for families." A family with younger children needs a built-in window for a nap or an afternoon rest, not a schedule that only remembers the kids are tired at three or four in the afternoon. The private morning boat on Lake Geneva runs under half a day, with the route and breakfast timing adjustable to a child's routine rather than the whole family bending to a fixed schedule.

## Pickup and waiting need to be sorted out in advance

Travelling with children means luggage, strollers, sudden bathroom stops — all real needs. The family experiences we arrange default to the driver waiting throughout, so parents don't have to calculate pickup timing themselves.

## Packing tips

- Carry your own snacks and water — many European museums and attractions don't have convenient options on hand.
- Bring a light jacket — indoor exhibition spaces can run noticeably cooler than outside.
- If your child naps, build a stretch into the itinerary where they can catch up in the car — that tends to work better than cancelling the nap outright and hoping the afternoon holds together.

## Frequently asked questions

**Below what age should we avoid in-depth museum commentary?** Generally under 5 is better suited to short, interactive activities; in-depth commentary tends to work better from around 5, once a child can follow a simplified narrative.

**What if the children travelling together span a wide age range?** Tell us the youngest and oldest ages and we'll find a middle ground on depth and pacing, or split the itinerary into segments handled differently by age group.

**How far ahead should family experiences be booked?** At least 2–3 weeks is a good baseline; for busier periods (summer holidays, Christmas and New Year) we'd suggest 1–2 months, to give us room to coordinate guides and venue slots.

If you're planning a European trip with children, tell us their exact ages and what interests them, and we'll judge which experiences genuinely fit and how to set the depth of commentary — rather than treating "family-friendly" as a catch-all label.
