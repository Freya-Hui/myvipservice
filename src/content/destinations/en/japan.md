Japan rewards a trip built around fewer places seen properly rather than a long list covered quickly — this is one destination where a slower pace genuinely changes what you take away from it.

## Getting In and Getting Around

Haneda Airport, closer to central Tokyo at roughly 30–40 minutes by car or monorail, has taken on more international routes in recent years and is generally the more convenient arrival point; Narita, further out at 60–90 minutes, still handles a large share of long-haul flights. Between Tokyo and Kyoto, the Shinkansen bullet train covers the distance in about two hours and fifteen minutes on the fastest Nozomi services, reliable enough that a same-day connection between the two cities is entirely workable.

## Tokyo and Kyoto, in Contrast

Tokyo is scale and speed — a city of distinct, self-contained neighborhoods (Ginza for luxury shopping, Roppongi for nightlife and contemporary art, Asakusa for the older city) that reward a few days spent going deep into two or three rather than skimming all of them. Kyoto moves at the opposite pace: former imperial capital, home to over a thousand temples and shrines, and best experienced early in the morning — Fushimi Inari's torii gate paths and the city's most photographed temples are markedly different places before the day's crowds arrive.

## Practical Notes

Cherry blossom (late March into early April, Tokyo generally a few days ahead of Kyoto) and autumn foliage (November) dates shift year to year and are hard to predict more than a few weeks out — keeping flexibility either side of planned travel dates makes it easier to align with the actual bloom. Japan remains more cash-reliant than many visitors expect, particularly outside major cities, and a traditional ryokan stay is worth booking well ahead, since the best-known properties have genuinely limited room counts.
